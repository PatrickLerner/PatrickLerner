Samira and I got married in Yerevan in August 2026.

We did it in Armenia because that is where we were living, and because doing it in Germany would have meant months of appointment roulette at a Standesamt. Armenia, once the documents existed, married us the working day after we filed, and had the apostille done inside a week.

Here is what actually happened, with the parts that cost time and money.

## You do not need a residence permit

Several law firm pages and the German embassy page say that at least one partner needs a valid Armenian residence permit, and that two foreigners need advance clearance from the Ministry of Justice. Neither is in the current law, and neither was true for us. We had no residence permit between us when we filed.

Do not restructure your plans around that claim. The official service page is [here](https://english.hartak.am/services/1227e954-c5b5-4791-b095-df9ded8c9655/offline/).

## Most English guides describe a law that no longer exists

The 2004 act was replaced in 2021. Guides still quote the old one. Things you will read that are simply wrong now:

- "Not earlier than the tenth day, not later than three months." The current rule is next working day at the earliest, six months at the latest.
- "Both parties must be present, a proxy is prohibited." A notarised application from the absent party works.
- "Registration takes ten to fifteen days." Registration is a same day database operation.

Anything written before mid 2021 sounds authoritative and is worse than useless.

## The documents are the whole job

What Armenia wants is proof that you are free to marry. Each side gets it differently, and that is where all the time goes.

**From Germany**, an erweiterte Meldebescheinigung, the extended registration certificate that states your current marital status, with an apostille. If you have been married before, the document ending that marriage comes with it.

Two things that look like shortcuts and are not. A birth certificate is useless here, because it says nothing about your status today. And the Ehefähigkeitszeugnis, the certificate of no impediment, is only issued if you hand your own authorities a pile of information about your intended spouse, which defeats the point of going abroad in the first place.

**From Iran**, a certificate of celibacy from the embassy in Yerevan. The embassy issues it already translated, and you then take it to the Armenian Ministry of Foreign Affairs, which confirms the embassy's work. No international shipping, no apostille from home. That is a far easier path than mine.

**Both of you** also need an official Armenian translation of your passport. Do not go hunting for an agency for this one. The registry office itself, the ՔԿԱԳ, which is the Armenian equivalent of a ZAGS or a Standesamt, does it for you at around 5,000 AMD per document.

If only one of you needs documents from a third country, plan the whole schedule around that person.

## What it cost

- **German documents**: certificates, apostille and express shipping to Yerevan, around 250 euros. Shipping alone was over 100.
- **Certified Armenian translation** of the German documents: 37,000 AMD, roughly 85 euros, notary fee included.
- **Passport translations**: around 5,000 AMD each, done at the registry office.
- **Iranian embassy certificate**: around 20,000 AMD, if memory serves.
- **Confirmation by the Armenian Ministry of Foreign Affairs**: around 5,000 AMD.
- **State duty for the registration**: 100,000 AMD. It is free only if both of you are Armenian. As soon as one foreigner is involved, you pay.
- **Apostille on the marriage certificate**: 7,000 AMD.
- **Ceremony**: an optional add-on, 20,000 to 60,000 AMD depending on venue and day. We skipped it.

That is around 665 euros all in, and closer to 560 once DHL refunded the shipping. No lawyer, no ceremony, no rings. The single biggest line is the state duty, and the second is getting two pieces of German paper into Armenia.

## Always get several translation quotes

The spread is absurd for identical work. Getting my German documents into Armenian: one agency wanted 43,000 AMD, another 32,000, both including the notary fee, a few streets apart. At pickup the second one charged 37,000 anyway, so treat a quote as an opening position.

The same going the other way. For the Armenian certificate into German, quotes ranged from about 50 euros from a sworn translator directly to over 140 from an agency. The direct translator was faster. Agencies are a middleman on a document that one person types.

## Published opening hours are not opening hours

We went to the Ministry of Foreign Affairs on a day everything said it was open until 6pm. The counter we needed ran 9:30 to 12:00. The whole trip was wasted, in August heat, with taxi drivers who accepted the ride and then drove the other way.

Go early. Treat every office as morning-only until proven otherwise.

## English at the counter is luck, not a right

The law has no language provision at all for this procedure. The general administrative law says proceedings run in Armenian, and that you bring your own translator if you need another language, but only if the office cannot handle you itself.

In practice it depends on who you get. Our clerk would not speak English. Not could not, would not. We asked another woman in the room, there for her own appointment, whether she would interpret for us. She agreed, then told us the clerk was simply being unpleasant, and we ended up redirected to a colleague who ran the whole process for us instead.

That was the only real friction of the day. Once we had someone willing it took minutes. She asked one date back from my paperwork, what surname Samira wanted, and when we wanted to marry. She was surprised we said the next day. That was the entire interview.

I understand not wanting to speak a foreign language in an official setting.

English does work here. It just sometimes takes a detour, and the detour is other people. Ask a younger person in the queue to translate. Nobody minds, and it is faster than arguing. If you want to skip the headache entirely, bring a friend who speaks Armenian. Russian is not the easy fallback you might expect, and may be harder than English.

Do not bother calling the Ministry of Justice line either. The recorded menu is in English, then you get forwarded to a human who will not want to speak it. Go in person.

## You almost certainly do not need a lawyer

We asked two. One quoted 1,300 dollars, of which 500 was just for reading our documents. Another wanted around 50 euros for a consultation.

For 200 to 300 euros I would happily have paid someone to run the errands. At 1,300 it is gouging. In the end it was not complicated enough to need anyone.

## Complain when the courier is late

DHL delivered our documents two days after the promised date, which nearly cost us the filing week. We complained, and they refunded the entire shipping cost even though the package did arrive.

Worth knowing before you swallow a late delivery quietly. Their customer service was the one part of this process that worked better than advertised.

## There is no wedding and there is no paper certificate

Nobody asks you to say anything. Nobody signs anything on the day. You sign once, when you file. On the registration day the state writes a record into a database and that is the marriage.

A ceremony exists as a paid checkbox on the form. We did not want one. The idea that a single day defines a marriage is a story you can buy, not a thing that is true.

The certificate is supposed to arrive by email. Ours never did. We went back and asked, and the woman printed it for us right there. Treat the email as optional. Once you hold the document, it carries a link and a QR code, and you can pull the digital original from there whenever you want.

That matters, because there is no wet ink original in Armenia. There is a digital document and there are printouts of it. Going to an office to have them print one is pointless, since you can print it yourself.

## Then get the apostille

You will almost certainly need the certificate apostilled for use abroad. That happens at the Ministry of Justice, after the registration.

Have them print the certificate once so you have a physical copy in hand, then walk across the street to the other building and file for the apostille. It costs 7,000 AMD and took two to three days for us. The apostille comes back by email, digital like everything else.

## Would I do it in Armenia again

Only because we were already there.

If you have a free choice, Georgia looks easier by every account I have heard. There is no reason to specifically pick Armenia unless you are Armenian, or you are standing in Armenia already.

But if you do pick Armenia, it is a good process. One working day from filing to married, a few more to the apostille, and the people handling it are reasonable and quick once you are in front of them. I would recommend it.
